About Me

I'm a final-year Computer Science student at Manchester Metropolitan University, graduating in 2027. I like the parts of building software that are easy to get wrong — money that has to balance, requests that mustn't run twice, and interfaces that still work properly with a keyboard or screen reader.

This site is where I document the things I build, the problems I run into, and what I learn along the way. You'll find my projects, technical write-ups, accessibility work, and updates as I continue developing my skills.

I've built a banking API with a double-entry ledger, row-locked transfers and idempotency keys, a real-time chat application, and this portfolio site itself. For the site, I also carried out an accessibility pass on the gallery rather than leaving it as a future improvement.

How I Got Here

While studying for my Extended Diploma in Engineering at college, I was originally considering mechanical or electrical engineering. The Arduino boards and small hardware projects on the course were what pulled me towards software: writing code that made something physical happen and seeing the result straight away.

I also became interested in financial markets and Forex trading bots. Building automated strategies meant turning ideas into rules, writing those rules into software, and seeing what happened when markets did not behave as expected. That interest eventually fed into NovaBank — a banking API designed around correctness, with a double-entry ledger, row locks and idempotency keys to handle transfers that race or are retried.

Alongside my degree, I work as an IT Advisor at Manchester Metropolitan University. It's hands-on support work: handling service desk tickets, PC imaging, SCCM software deployment, and helping staff and students with hardware, software, accounts and network issues. A lot of those problems do not have a straightforward answer, so the job has taught me to diagnose problems under pressure and explain the solution clearly.

How I Work

Outside college and university, if a laptop, phone or tablet stopped working, I would usually try to figure out what was wrong before replacing it. I still approach software in much the same way: if I don't understand something, I want to take it apart and work out why.

That curiosity is probably what has kept me interested in software. I enjoy building things, but I'm just as interested in understanding what happens underneath — especially when something goes wrong.
